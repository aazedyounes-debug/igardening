Calathea: Care, Varieties and Maintenance Tips

By iGardening Team March 30, 2026 14 min read

The calathea is undeniably one of the most spectacular and coveted houseplants among plant enthusiasts. With its mesmerizing geometric leaf patterns, colors ranging from emerald green to deep purple, and its fascinating "prayer plant" behavior, the calathea transforms any interior into a true tropical garden. But let's be honest: the calathea also has a reputation for being a fussy plant, demanding when it comes to water quality, humidity, and light. This comprehensive guide will give you all the keys to taming this tropical beauty and keeping it thriving for many years.

Quick Facts - Calathea
  • Family: Marantaceae
  • Origin: Tropical forests of South America (mainly Brazil)
  • Adult size indoors: 40 cm to 1 meter depending on variety
  • Growth rate: Moderate
  • Difficulty: Moderate to difficult
  • Toxicity: Non-toxic to pets and children
  • Special feature: Nyctinastic movement (leaves fold up at night)

The Prayer Movement: A Fascinating Phenomenon

Before diving into care details, let's talk about what makes the calathea truly unique in the plant world: its nyctinastic movement, commonly called the "prayer movement." Every evening at dusk, calathea leaves slowly rise upward, like hands joined in prayer. In the morning, they lower again to capture maximum light.

This movement is controlled by a small organ at the base of each leaf called the pulvinus, which acts as a hydraulic joint. Variations in water pressure within the pulvinus cells cause the petiole and leaf to move. This mechanism is regulated by the plant's internal biological clock (circadian rhythm), which responds to changes in light.

This movement is also an excellent indicator of your calathea's health. A healthy plant actively moves every day. If your calathea stops moving, or if its leaves remain constantly curled or upright, it's an alarm signal indicating environmental stress: lack of water, too much light, or insufficient humidity.

Tip: If you want to observe the prayer movement in fast motion, film your calathea in time-lapse over 24 hours. The result is absolutely fascinating and will give you a new appreciation for this extraordinary plant.

The Most Popular Calathea Varieties

The Calathea genus (recently reclassified as Goeppertia by botanists, though the name Calathea remains the most widely used in horticulture) includes dozens of cultivated species and varieties. Here are the most popular and easiest to find at garden centers.

Calathea orbifolia

Calathea orbifolia is considered by many to be the most elegant of all calatheas. Its large, round leaves (reaching up to 30 cm in diameter) display alternating silver-green and dark green stripes, creating a subtle and sophisticated visual effect. Unlike other varieties with highly contrasting patterns, the orbifolia charms with its softness and restraint. It is one of the largest varieties, reaching 80 cm to 1 meter in height indoors. It is also considered one of the easiest calatheas to grow, making it an excellent starting point for beginners.

Calathea medallion (veitchiana)

Calathea medallion is probably the best-known and most widely sold variety at garden centers. And for good reason: its oval leaves display a spectacular medallion pattern of dark green surrounded by light green, with prominent veins and a deep purple underside. The contrast between the top and bottom of the leaf is striking. Medium-sized (50 to 70 cm), the medallion is compact and bushy, perfect for decorating a coffee table or shelf. It is moderately demanding in terms of care, primarily requiring good humidity and watering with non-calcareous water.

Calathea lancifolia (rattlesnake plant)

Calathea lancifolia, nicknamed the "rattlesnake plant" due to its patterns, stands out with its long, narrow leaves (up to 45 cm) shaped like lances. The foliage features alternating dark green oval spots of increasing size on a wavy light green background, effectively evoking a snake pattern. The undersides of the leaves are a beautiful dark purple. The lancifolia is known for being one of the hardiest and most tolerant calatheas. It handles slightly drier air and occasional watering mistakes better than others, making it an excellent choice for beginners who want to try calatheas without risking quick disappointment.

Calathea ornata (pinstripe plant)

Calathea ornata, or the "pinstripe plant," is immediately recognizable thanks to its fine pink or white perfectly parallel lines crossing its dark green leaves, as if painted by a meticulous artist. In young plants, these lines are a vivid pink that fades to cream or white as the leaf matures. The undersides of the leaves are dark purple. The ornata can reach 60 to 90 cm in height and is of medium difficulty. It is slightly more demanding in humidity than the lancifolia but less finicky than the white fusion.

Calathea makoyana (peacock plant)

Calathea makoyana, called the "peacock plant," perfectly lives up to its nickname. Its oval, thin, almost translucent leaves display an extraordinary peacock feather pattern: dark green ovals on a pale green, almost silvery background, with reddish veins visible through the leaf. It is one of the most decorative calatheas but also one of the most delicate. It requires consistently high humidity and reacts very poorly to hard water and drafts.

Calathea white fusion

Calathea white fusion is the social media star and the most spectacular variety, with its leaves marbled in white, green, and lilac. Each leaf is unique, like an abstract work of art. However, it is also the most demanding and most difficult calathea to maintain. The white variegation contains less chlorophyll, making the plant more sensitive to direct light, humidity fluctuations, and water quality. Reserve this variety for experienced growers who have already mastered the basics of calathea care.

Light: Finding the Right Balance

In their natural habitat, calatheas grow on the floor of dense tropical forests, beneath a thick tree canopy that filters out nearly all direct sunlight. This bright shade condition is what you need to replicate indoors.

Ideal lighting

The calathea needs moderate to bright indirect light. It thrives in spots where it receives abundant diffused light without ever being touched by direct sunlight. Ideal locations include:

  • Near a north or east-facing window: Soft light all day long, ideal for most varieties.
  • 1-2 meters from a south or west-facing window: Enough light without risk of burning. A sheer curtain may be needed in summer.
  • In a bright but not sunny room: Calatheas adapt well to offices and artificially lit rooms, provided the light is sufficient.

Signs of inadequate lighting

  • Too much light: Leaves become discolored, pale, and lose their contrasting patterns. Dry brown spots (burns) appear. Leaves curl to protect themselves. Variegated varieties (white fusion) are the first affected.
  • Not enough light: Growth slows significantly. New leaves are smaller and less colorful. Stems elongate and lean toward the light source. The prayer movement becomes less pronounced.

The calathea is one of the few houseplants that truly tolerates medium light levels, making it an excellent choice for less sunny rooms. However, it will not survive in total darkness. If you don't have sufficient natural light, a full-spectrum LED grow light placed 30-50 cm above the plant for 10 to 12 hours a day can perfectly compensate.

Watering: A Sensitive Subject

If the calathea has a reputation for being difficult, it's largely because of its watering requirements. Not only does the watering frequency need to be adjusted, but water quality is equally crucial. Calatheas are particularly sensitive to chlorine, fluoride, and calcium found in tap water.

What water to use?

This is the most important point for successfully growing a calathea. Tap water, in many areas, is too loaded with minerals and chemicals for this sensitive plant. Here are your options, from best to worst:

  1. Rainwater: The ideal solution, free and naturally soft. Collect it in a clean container and use it at room temperature.
  2. Filtered water: A filter pitcher like Brita removes most chlorine and calcium. This is the most practical everyday solution.
  3. Reverse osmosis water: Excellent quality, available at pet stores (aquarium section) or with a home reverse osmosis system.
  4. Rested tap water: If you have no other option, let tap water sit in an open container for 24 to 48 hours. Chlorine evaporates naturally. However, calcium and fluoride remain.

Watering frequency and technique

The calathea likes soil that is consistently moist but never waterlogged. It's a delicate balance that requires observation and practice:

  • In summer: Water as soon as the top centimeter of soil feels dry to the touch, usually every 3 to 5 days.
  • In winter: Reduce the frequency, allowing the top 2-3 centimeters to dry out. Watering every 7 to 10 days is often sufficient.

Always water with lukewarm water (room temperature), never cold water that can shock tropical roots. Water slowly and evenly over the entire soil surface until water begins to flow from the drainage holes. Empty the saucer after 15 minutes to prevent roots from sitting in standing water.

Signs of improper watering
  • Underwatering: Leaves curling inward (like a cigar), drooping and limp leaves, dry brown edges, prayer movement stops.
  • Overwatering: Yellow and limp leaves, blackened stem bases, musty smell, brown water spots on leaves, root rot.
  • Poor water quality: Leaf tips and edges gradually turning brown, whitish deposits on soil surface, slowed growth.

Humidity: The Key Success Factor

Ambient humidity is arguably the most critical factor for a calathea's well-being. Native to tropical forests where relative humidity often exceeds 80%, the calathea suffers considerably in our heated interiors where humidity can drop below 30% in winter.

What humidity level to aim for?

  • Ideal: 60% to 80% relative humidity
  • Acceptable: 50% to 60% (most varieties adapt)
  • Critical: Below 40%, problems appear quickly (brown edges, curling leaves)

How to increase humidity

Here are the most effective methods, ranked by effectiveness:

  1. A humidifier: By far the most effective and reliable solution. A cool mist (or ultrasonic) humidifier placed near your calatheas will maintain a constant humidity level. Aim for 60% minimum. It's an investment that will literally transform the health of your tropical plants.
  2. A humidity tray: Fill a wide tray with clay pebbles or gravel and add water to halfway up the pebbles. Place your pots on top, making sure the bottom of the pot doesn't touch the water. Evaporation creates a humid microclimate around the plants.
  3. Grouping plants: Gather your tropical plants (calatheas, ferns, marantas, fittonias) to create a natural humidity island through collective evapotranspiration.
  4. A bright bathroom: If you have a bathroom with a window, it's the dream location for a calathea. The naturally high humidity and constant warmth are ideal.
  5. Misting: Spray the leaves once or twice daily with non-calcareous water. This method is the least effective as the effect is very temporary (30 minutes maximum), but it provides a useful supplement to other methods.

Good to know: Investing in a hygrometer (thermometer-hygrometer) is essential when growing calatheas. This inexpensive device ($10-15) will let you precisely measure humidity levels and adjust your efforts accordingly. Place it near your plants, not at the other end of the room.

The Ideal Soil Mix for Calathea

The calathea needs a soil mix that retains moisture well while remaining airy and well-drained. Soil that is too compact will retain too much water and cause root rot. A mix that drains too quickly will dry out too fast and stress the plant. Here is our recommended recipe:

  • 40% quality indoor plant potting mix
  • 20% perlite (for aeration and drainage)
  • 20% coco coir (retains moisture without compacting)
  • 10% fine pine bark (aeration)
  • 10% vermiculite (water retention and aeration)

This blend offers a good balance between moisture retention and drainage. The soil should remain loose and never compact into a hard block. If you notice that watering runs directly down the sides of the pot without penetrating the soil, it's a sign that it's time to repot.

Temperature: Consistent Warmth Without Drafts

As a tropical plant, the calathea loves warmth and hates sudden temperature changes. Ideal conditions are:

  • Ideal temperature: 18°C to 25°C (65°F to 77°F) year-round
  • Absolute minimum: 15°C (59°F) (below this, the plant suffers and may die)
  • Maximum tolerated: 30°C (86°F) (beyond this, increase watering and humidity)

The calathea's temperature enemies are drafts, whether hot or cold. Keep your plant away from radiators, air conditioning vents, doors, and windows that are frequently opened. A cold draft in winter can cause immediate browning of the most exposed leaves.

Fertilizing Your Calathea

Calatheas are not heavy feeders, but regular, moderate fertilization during the growing season supports their development and improves leaf coloring.

When and how to fertilize

  • March to October: Fertilize once a month with a liquid fertilizer for green plants, diluted to half the recommended dose on the packaging. Calatheas are sensitive to over-fertilizing -- it's better to under-dose than over-dose.
  • November to February: Stop all fertilization. The plant is in its dormant period and does not need extra nutrients.
  • Always on moist soil: Never apply fertilizer to dry soil, as you risk burning the roots. Water first with plain water, then apply the fertilizer solution.

Signs of over-fertilization

Excess fertilizer causes mineral salt buildup in the soil, which damages roots and shows up as brown tips, a crusty white deposit on the surface, and slowed growth. If this happens, flush the soil thoroughly by running water through it for several minutes, and suspend fertilization for 2 months.

Repotting Your Calathea

The calathea has a relatively modest root system and doesn't need to be repotted frequently. Repotting every 2 years is generally sufficient, preferably in spring (March-April).

When to repot?

  • Roots are growing out of the drainage holes
  • The soil is compacted and no longer drains properly
  • The plant dries out very quickly between waterings
  • Growth has noticeably slowed despite good conditions

How to repot

  1. Choose a pot that is 2 to 3 cm larger maximum. A pot that's too large retains too much moisture and promotes root rot. Make sure it has drainage holes.
  2. Water the plant the day before to make removal easier.
  3. Gently remove the plant and shake off the old soil. Inspect the roots: cut any that are brown, mushy, or foul-smelling.
  4. If the plant forms a dense clump with multiple stems, this is also the ideal time to divide it and get new plants.
  5. Fill the new pot one-third with fresh soil mix, place the plant in the center, and fill in around it. Don't pack too firmly.
  6. Water thoroughly with filtered water and place the plant in a warm, humid spot away from direct light for one to two weeks while the roots adjust.

Propagating Your Calathea

Unlike many houseplants, the calathea cannot be propagated by stem or leaf cuttings. The only reliable method of propagation is division of clumps, done during repotting in spring.

  1. Remove the plant from its pot and gently remove the soil from the roots.
  2. Identify the different clumps that make up the plant. Each clump should have at least 2-3 leaves and its own root system.
  3. Separate the clumps by gently pulling apart or cutting with a clean knife if the roots are too tangled.
  4. Repot each division in an individual pot with fresh soil mix.
  5. Keep the new plants in a very humid environment (clear plastic bag over the pot or glass cloche) for 2 to 3 weeks to encourage recovery.

Common Problems and Solutions

Brown edges and tips (the number one problem)

This is by far the most common problem with calatheas and it almost always has the same cause: insufficient humidity and/or poor water quality. Browning starts at the leaf tips, then spreads to the edges. Affected leaves will not heal (dead tissue stays brown), but you can stop the progression by addressing the causes:

  • Switch to filtered water or rainwater for watering
  • Increase ambient humidity to 60% minimum (humidifier)
  • Move the plant away from any direct heat source (radiator, heater)
  • Make sure the soil doesn't dry out completely between waterings

You can trim brown edges with clean scissors following the natural shape of the leaf to improve the plant's appearance.

Curling leaves

When calathea leaves curl inward like a cigar, it's a defense mechanism against water loss. The plant reduces its exposed surface area to limit evaporation. Possible causes include:

  • Soil too dry: Check the soil moisture and water if needed. The leaves should unfurl within hours of a thorough watering.
  • Air too dry: Increase ambient humidity.
  • Too much light: Move the plant away from the window or add a sheer curtain.
  • Hot draft: Move the plant away from the radiator or heat source.

Yellow leaves

Yellowing leaves can have several causes:

  • Overwatering: The most common cause. Check that the pot drains properly and the saucer is emptied. Reduce watering frequency.
  • Natural aging: The lowest and oldest leaves naturally yellow and die to make room for new ones. An occasional leaf is normal.
  • Too much sun: Overly intense light bleaches the leaves. Move the plant to a less bright spot.
  • Nutrient deficiency: If you haven't fertilized in a long time, nitrogen deficiency can cause general yellowing. Resume fertilizing.

Brown spots on leaves

Unlike brown edges (related to humidity), brown spots in the middle of leaves often have other causes:

  • Sunburn: Dry, crunchy spots with irregular shapes. Move the plant away from direct sun.
  • Fungal infections: Brown spots with a yellow halo, often soft and moist. Caused by excess moisture on foliage combined with poor air circulation. Remove affected leaves, improve ventilation, and avoid wetting leaves in the evening.
  • Water spots: Water droplets left on leaves under bright light can act as magnifying glasses and burn plant tissue. Dry the leaves after misting if the plant is in a bright spot.

Spider mites and other pests

Calatheas are unfortunately susceptible to several pests, especially when the air is dry:

  • Spider mites: The calathea's number one enemy. These tiny arachnids thrive in dry, warm conditions. You can spot them by the fine webs under the leaves and small yellowish dots on top. The best prevention is maintaining good humidity. In case of infestation, treat with diluted insecticidal soap or neem oil, focusing on the undersides of leaves.
  • Mealybugs: White cottony clusters in leaf axils and under leaves. Remove them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol and treat with insecticidal soap.
  • Thrips: Tiny elongated insects that leave silvery streaks and black dots on leaves. Treat with insecticidal soap or neem oil.
Problem prevention

The best defense against calathea problems is prevention: maintain consistently high humidity (60%+), water with filtered or rainwater, avoid direct sun and drafts, and regularly inspect the undersides of leaves. A well-maintained calathea in good conditions is naturally resistant to pests and diseases.

Seasonal Tips

Spring-Summer

This is the active growing period. New leaves appear regularly, rolled up like cigars before slowly unfurling (a sight not to be missed!). Gradually increase watering and begin monthly fertilization. This is the ideal time to repot and divide clumps. Watch for pests, especially spider mites as temperatures rise. Protect the plant from direct summer sun.

Fall-Winter

Growth slows significantly. Reduce watering and stop fertilizing. This is the most critical period for calatheas because of the dry air caused by central heating. Double your efforts to maintain humidity: group plants together, use the humidifier and humidity trays. Keep the plant away from radiators. Compensate for reduced light by moving the plant closer to a window, without exposing it to cold drafts.

In Summary

The calathea is a demanding but incredibly rewarding plant. Its spectacular leaves and daily prayer movement make it a living, fascinating presence in any interior. The keys to success are simple to remember: moderate indirect light, regular watering with filtered or rainwater, high ambient humidity (60% minimum), and consistent temperature without drafts. If you follow these four pillars, your calathea will reward you with lush foliage and dazzling colors. Don't hesitate to start with the easiest varieties (lancifolia, orbifolia) before tackling the more delicate ones (white fusion, makoyana). Every calathea you successfully care for is a victory that builds your confidence as an indoor gardener.
